Statics, Lesson 1

Friction

Friction is the force that keeps a ladder on the wall and a crate on a ramp. In this lesson you will draw free-body diagrams that include friction, test whether a body stays in equilibrium, and decide whether it will slip or tip first.

Try it before you read anything. Raise the ramp until the block moves. Then switch to the tall block, or to sandpaper, and see whether it moves the same way.

What you will be able to do

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish static, impending, and kinetic friction states for a body in contact with a dry surface.
  2. Draw a free-body diagram that shows the friction force direction and the location x of the resultant normal force.
  3. Classify a dry-friction problem by comparing the number of unknowns with the available equilibrium and friction equations.
  4. Determine whether slipping or tipping governs when a force is applied to a body resting on a rough surface.
  5. Measure a coefficient of static friction using the angle of impending slip on an inclined plane.

What is in the lesson

  1. Why friction matters
  2. Three states of friction, friction direction, and angled pushes and pulls
  3. Free-body diagrams with friction, and where the normal force acts
  4. Slip or tip? A procedure, with unlimited practice
  5. Three types of friction problems
  6. The painter’s ladder
  7. Friction on an incline

What you need first

Free-body diagrams, the three equations of equilibrium for a rigid body in two dimensions, the moment of a force, weight from mass (W = mg), and right-triangle trigonometry. The lesson opens with two questions so you can check, and “Need a refresher?” buttons throughout open a short review of each idea at the point you need it.

Outcomes this lesson develops

  • Quantitative Knowledge and Skills (Institutional Learning Outcome 2). You solve equilibrium and friction equations and interpret the result.
  • Scientific Knowledge and Reasoning (Institutional Learning Outcome 3). In the lab you predict, measure, and compare.
  • Critical Thinking (Institutional Learning Outcome 11). You assume a failure mode, solve, and test your own assumption.
