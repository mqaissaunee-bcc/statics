Statics, Lesson 1, Lab

Friction on an incline

Measuring Static Friction and Predicting Slip or Tip on an Incline. You will measure a coefficient of static friction with a ramp and a protractor, then predict what a tall block will do and find out whether you were right.

About 35 minutes in class, in groups of two or three. Your entries on this page are saved on this device.

Objectives

  • Measure μs for two or three pairs of surfaces from the angle of impending slip.
  • Predict, then test, whether a tall block slips or tips first.

The idea

Set a block on a ramp at angle θ. Its weight has a component W sin θ down the slope, which friction must balance, and a component W cos θ into the slope, which the normal force balances. So F = W sin θ and N = W cos θ. At the moment of impending slip, F = μsN, and the weight cancels:

μs = tan φs φs is the angle at which the block just begins to slip

A block of base b and height H tips when its weight’s line of action passes the downhill edge of its base:

tan θtip = b/H

Whichever angle is smaller is the one the block reaches first.

Try the simulated ramp before you touch the real one. The friction values in it are for illustration only; yours will differ.

Equipment

  • Adjustable ramp or hinged board
  • Two wood blocks: one low, one tall (height about three times the base)
  • Two or three ramp surfaces, for example bare board, felt, and sandpaper
  • Protractor or a phone inclinometer app
  • Ruler and masking tape

Safety

  • Keep fingers clear of the hinge and of the block’s path down the ramp.
  • Tape a soft stop at the lower end of the ramp so blocks do not leave the table.
  • Use blocks under 1 kg.
  • Wear closed-toe shoes.
  • Hold or prop the ramp so it cannot drop when you let go.

Procedure

  1. Measure the tall block’s base and height. Enter them below.
  2. Place the low block on the first surface with the ramp flat. Mark its starting spot with tape so every trial starts from the same place.
  3. Raise the ramp slowly and steadily. Stop the instant the block begins to slip, and record the angle.
  4. Repeat for five trials, then change surfaces.
  5. The page computes μs = tan φs for each trial, with the mean and range. Compute one by hand to confirm you agree with it.
  6. Before you test the tall block, read the predictions in Table 2 and discuss them. Does your group agree?
  7. Test the tall block on each surface, three times each. Record what it did.
  8. Write your memo.

Memo

Write half a page, or record a two-minute explanation if your instructor allows it. Address these four points:

  1. Your measured μs for each surface, with its range.
  2. For each surface, whether the tall block did what you predicted.
  3. Two specific sources of error in your measurements, and which direction each would push your result.
  4. One sentence on this claim: “A surface with more friction is always the safer choice.” Use what the tall block did.
