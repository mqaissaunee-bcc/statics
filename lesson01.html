Statics, Lesson 1

Friction

Until now, most of our problems came with supports that held firm. This lesson asks a different question: will the body stay where it is at all? The answer depends on friction, and on something a particle model hides, which is where the floor pushes back.

About 50 minutes, plus the 25-minute activity. Your progress on the checks is saved on this device.

Before you start

Two questions on ideas this lesson leans on. If either one is shaky, review moments and rigid-body equilibrium before going on.

1. Why friction matters

It is tempting to think of friction as waste: heat, wear, and lost energy. Sometimes it is. But take friction away and you cannot walk, a car cannot brake or steer, a ladder slides out from the wall, and every bolted joint in a steel building comes loose. Engineers spend as much effort getting enough friction as they do getting rid of it.

The friction in this lesson is dry friction, also called Coulomb friction: the friction between solid surfaces with no lubricant between them (Hibbeler, 2022).

2. Three states of friction

Push gently on a heavy desk and it does not move. You are not weak; the floor is pushing back on the desk with a friction force exactly as large as your push. Push harder and friction grows to match. This is static friction, and the key fact about it is that it is a reaction. It takes whatever value equilibrium requires, up to a limit:

F ≤ μsN

Here F is the friction force, N is the normal force, and μs is the coefficient of static friction, a number with no units that depends on the two surfaces in contact.

At the limit, the body is on the verge of slipping. This state is called impending motion, and it is the only time you may write the friction force as an equation:

Fmax = μsN only at impending motion

Push past the limit and the body slides. Friction drops to the kinetic friction force, Fk = μkN, where μk is the coefficient of kinetic friction. For most pairs of surfaces μs is larger than μk, which is why a heavy garbage bag is harder to start dragging than to keep dragging (Beer et al., 2019).

Two details people get wrong

The coefficient is not a force. μs is a ratio. The force needed to start a body sliding on a level floor is μsN, which depends on how hard the surfaces are pressed together.

Friction does not always oppose the motion of the object. It opposes sliding between the two surfaces, or the tendency to slide. When you walk, your shoe pushes backward on the floor. Friction on your shoe points forward, the same direction you are moving. To find the direction, ask which way the surface would slide if friction vanished, and point friction the other way.

3. Free-body diagrams with friction

In a physics course a crate is usually a particle, and the normal force is drawn through its center. A real crate is a rigid body, and the floor’s push is spread across the whole base. We replace that spread-out push with a single resultant N, and its location is one of the unknowns.

Consider a crate of weight W and base width a, pushed by a horizontal force P at height h. Four forces act on it: P, W straight down through the center of gravity, friction F along the floor, and N at some distance x from the centerline. Equilibrium gives:

ΣFx = 0:   P − F = 0 so F = P

ΣFy = 0:   N − W = 0 so N = W

ΣMO = 0:   Nx − Ph = 0 so x = Ph/W, taking moments about O, the middle of the base

The push tries to rotate the crate forward. The only thing that can resist is N moving toward the front edge. The harder or higher you push, the farther it moves. But N cannot act outside the base. When x reaches a/2 the crate is about to tip.

So equilibrium needs two conditions at once:

  • No slipping: F ≤ μsN
  • No tipping: x ≤ a/2

Use Figure 2 to try three things. Raise P slowly and watch N walk toward the edge. Lower the push height and see the tipping limit move away. Then raise μs and watch a crate that used to slide start to tip instead.

More grip is not always better

It is easy to assume the stronger material, or the grippier surface, is always the better choice. A crate that slides when it is pushed too hard gives a warning and stays upright. Put it on a high-friction mat and the same push may tip it over instead. Tires, brake pads, and bearings are all chosen as trade-offs among grip, wear, heat, and cost. The right amount of friction depends on what the design has to do.

4. Slip or tip? A procedure

When you do not know which failure comes first, assume one, solve, and check the other.

  1. Draw the free-body diagram. Show F opposing the tendency to slide and N at an unknown distance x.
  2. Assume one kind of impending motion. For slipping, add F = μsN. For tipping, set x = a/2.
  3. Solve the three equilibrium equations with that one extra condition.
  4. Check the other condition. If you assumed slipping, is x ≤ a/2? If you assumed tipping, is F ≤ μsN?
  5. If the check fails, your assumption was wrong. Switch and solve again. The motion that needs the smaller force governs.

Notice how little of this is arithmetic. The equations take a few lines. The engineering is in the diagram, the assumption, and the check, and that is where your work is graded.

Worked Example A: horizontal push, US customary units

A crate weighs W = 250 lb and has a base 3 ft wide. A worker pushes horizontally at a height of 5 ft. The coefficient of static friction is 0.40. What is the largest push that causes no motion?

Show the solution

Assume slipping. N = W = 250 lb, so

P = F = μsN = (0.40)(250 lb) = 100 lb

Check tipping.

x = Ph/W = (100 lb)(5 ft)/(250 lb) = 2.0 ft

But a/2 = 1.5 ft. The normal force would have to act outside the base, which is impossible. The assumption fails: the crate tips before it slips.

Assume tipping. Set x = 1.5 ft.

P = Wx/h = (250 lb)(1.5 ft)/(5 ft) = 75 lb

Check slipping. F = P = 75 lb, which is less than μsN = 100 lb. The check passes.

Answer. The largest push is 75 lb, and tipping governs.

Worked Example B: angled push, SI units

A uniform crate has a mass of 20 kg and a base 0.8 m wide. A force P = 80 N pushes on it at 30° below the horizontal. The force is applied 0.4 m to the left of the crate’s centerline and 0.2 m above the floor. The coefficient of static friction is 0.3. Does the crate stay in equilibrium? (After Hibbeler, 2022, Example 8.1.)

Show the solution

Here nothing says motion is impending, so there are three unknowns (F, N, x) and three equilibrium equations. Solve first, then check both limits. W = (20 kg)(9.81 m/s²) = 196.2 N.

ΣFx = 0:   (80 N) cos 30° − F = 0,   F = 69.3 N

ΣFy = 0:   N − (80 N) sin 30° − 196.2 N = 0,   N = 236.2 N

ΣMO = 0:   (40 N)(0.4 m) − (69.3 N)(0.2 m) + Nx = 0,   x = −0.00908 m

Check tipping. The negative sign means N acts about 9 mm to the left of the centerline. That is far inside the 0.4 m half-width, so the crate does not tip.

Check slipping. Fmax = μsN = (0.3)(236.2 N) = 70.9 N. The 69.3 N required is less than that, so the crate does not slip.

Answer. The crate stays in equilibrium, though it is close to slipping. Notice that pushing downward raised N, and with it the available friction.

5. Three types of friction problems

Once the free-body diagrams are drawn, count the unknowns and compare them with the equations you have: three equilibrium equations per rigid body, plus one friction equation for each contact where slipping is impending (Hibbeler, 2022).

Table 1Types of Dry-Friction Problems
TypeCountWhat you do
No apparent impending motionUnknowns = equilibrium equationsSolve with equilibrium alone, then check F ≤ μsN at every contact. Worked Example B is this type.
Impending motion at all points of contactUnknowns = equilibrium equations + friction equationsUse F = μsN at every contact and solve everything together.
Impending motion at some points of contactUnknowns < equilibrium equations + possible friction or tipping equationsSeveral motions are possible. Assume one, solve, and check, as in the slip-or-tip procedure. Worked Example A is this type.

Practice: a bar against a wall

A uniform bar weighing 100 N leans against a wall. The coefficient of static friction is 0.3 at the floor (point A) and 0.4 at the wall (point B). Find the smallest angle θ between the bar and the floor at which the bar will not slip. Work it on paper before opening the solution.

Show the solution

Slipping is impending at both contacts, so this is the second type: five unknowns (NA, FA, NB, FB, θ), three equilibrium equations, and two friction equations. The bottom of the bar tends to slide away from the wall, so FA points toward the wall. The top tends to slide down, so FB points up.

FA = 0.3NA,   FB = 0.4NB

ΣFx = 0:   NB = FA = 0.3NA

ΣFy = 0:   NA + 0.4(0.3NA) = 100 N,   NA = 89.3 N

So NB = 26.8 N and FB = 10.7 N. Taking moments about A for a bar of length L:

(100 N)(L/2) cos θ = (26.8 N)L sin θ + (10.7 N)L cos θ

tan θ = (50 − 10.7)/26.8 = 1.467,   θ = 55.7°

The length cancels. At any angle steeper than 55.7° the bar stays put.

Summary

  • Static friction is a reaction. It equals whatever equilibrium requires, up to μsN.
  • Write F = μsN only when motion is impending. Otherwise, solve for F and check it.
  • Friction opposes sliding between surfaces, which is not always the direction opposite the body’s motion.
  • On a rigid body, N shifts toward the edge the body would tip about. Tipping is impending when N reaches that edge.
  • When slipping and tipping are both possible, assume one, solve, and check the other.

Key terms

Dry (Coulomb) friction
Friction between unlubricated solid surfaces.
Normal force, N
The resultant contact force perpendicular to the surface.
Static friction force, F
The tangential force that prevents sliding. It takes whatever value equilibrium requires, up to a limit: F ≤ μsN.
Maximum static friction, Fmax
μsN, reached only at impending motion.
Impending motion
The state in which a body is on the verge of slipping.
Kinetic friction force, Fk
μkN, acting while surfaces slide.
Coefficients of static and kinetic friction, μs and μk
Dimensionless ratios that depend on the pair of surfaces. Typically μs is greater than μk.
Slipping
Sliding at the contact surface.
Tipping
Rotation about an edge, which begins when the normal force reaches that edge (x = a/2 for a base of width a).
Angle of static friction, φs
The incline angle at impending slip, where tan φs = μs. You will use this in the lab.
